1 · Foundations

What is a digital twin?

A digital twin is a virtual representation of one specific physical thing, connected to it by data throughout its life, so that the virtual side can explain, predict and improve what happens on the physical side.

The idea goes back to Michael Grieves' product lifecycle management lectures in the early 2000s. NASA and the U.S. Air Force made it widely known when they described a twin of an aircraft that would mirror the life of its flying counterpart (Glaessgen & Stargel, 2012). Grieves and Vickers (2017) describe three parts. Click each one:

One specific thing

A twin belongs to one serial number, one building or one patient, not to a product type. Two turbines of the same model have two twins, because they stand in different winds and wear differently.

For its whole life

The twin is not built for a single study and then archived. It keeps a history: how the object was designed, built, operated, repaired and changed.

To support decisions

A twin earns its cost only when it changes a decision: when to service, how to operate, what to design next. A copy that nobody acts on is only a picture.

A broader definition. The U.S. National Academies (2024) describe a digital twin as a set of virtual information constructs that mimics a physical system, is updated dynamically with data from it, has a predictive capability and informs decisions that realise value. They stress that the two-way interaction between virtual and physical is central.

2 · Digital twin technology

What technology makes a twin work?

"Digital twin" is the concept. Digital twin technology is the chain of hardware and software that keeps the virtual model in step with the real object and carries decisions back. No single product does all of it. Click a layer to see what it does and which methods it uses.

Data flows up the stack from the object to the model. Decisions flow back down to the object. Remove the top layer and you have a monitoring system; remove the bottom one and you have a simulation.

3 · A precise vocabulary

Digital model, digital shadow, digital twin

Many things called "digital twins" are not. Kritzinger et al. (2018) proposed a simple test: how does data move between the object and the model, and is it automatic? Switch between the three levels.

4 · A common confusion

How is a twin different from a simulation?

Both use models and both answer "what happens if…?". The difference is not the mathematics. It is what the model is tied to, and how long it lives.

Simulation modelDigital twin
What it representsA class of objects or a design idea ("a 5 MW turbine")One identified object ("turbine WTG-0427 at site B")
LifespanOne study: set up, run, read, closePersistent: lives as long as the object, often longer
InputsAssumed scenarios chosen by the analystLaws of physics plus the object's own measured data
Relation to realityValidated once, then trustedChecked against reality again and again, and recalibrated
MemoryNone between runsA history: as-designed, as-built, as-operated, as-maintained
Direction of informationModel → report → peopleObject ⇄ model, with decisions returning to the object
Typical question"Will this design survive a 50-year storm?""Given what this turbine has lived through, when will its main bearing fail, and what should we do today?"

They are not rivals. A twin runs simulations all the time. Simulation is one of the twin's tools; the twin adds identity, memory and a live connection. A good way to put it: a simulation is a question you ask once; a twin is a model that keeps answering as the object changes.

5 · Phase 0: physics first

Why can a model be a twin before anything is built?

Before a turbine, bridge or engine exists, there is no sensor data. Yet engineers already test it virtually in storms, overloads and failures. This works because the model's first "data" are not measurements but the laws of nature and the constants they contain. A physics-based model does not look up answers; it computes them, step by step, from principles that hold everywhere.

1 · CONSTANTS
g = 9.81 m/s²
Esteel ≈ 210 GPa
ρsteel ≈ 7850 kg/m³

Measured once by science, valid for every object made of that material.

2 · LAWS
F = m·a
energy is conserved

Conservation of mass, momentum and energy; constitutive laws such as Hooke's law.

3 · EQUATIONS
m·ẍ + c·ẋ + k·x = F(t)

Laws applied to the design's geometry give differential equations, often partial differential equations over space and time.

4 · SOLVER
xn+1 = xn + Δt · …

A numerical solver (finite elements, finite volumes, time stepping) advances the state in small steps, so conditions can change during the run.

Because the solver steps through time, the environment can be dynamic: wind can gust, a load can double halfway through, a support can settle. That is why the model can answer what-if questions in a changing environment long before a prototype exists. Try it in the live lab below.

Is it really a "twin" yet? Two scholarly views

Yes: the Digital Twin Prototype

Grieves and Vickers (2017) include a Digital Twin Prototype (DTP): the designs, analyses and processes needed to realise a product, created before the physical product exists. In this view the twin is born first and the physical object is later "made to match" it.

Not yet: it is a digital model

In the stricter classification of Kritzinger et al. (2018), a model with no automatic data link to a real object is a digital model. By that test, the Phase 0 model becomes a shadow and then a twin only once the built object is connected.

How to reconcile them. At Phase 0, the virtual half of the twin exists and already does useful work, while the physical half and the connection do not exist yet. Calling it a twin is justified when the model is built to become the twin of the object that will be produced: same identity, same equations, same data structures, carried forward by the digital thread. In an exam or a thesis, say which definition you use. Students who can explain both views, and why they differ, understand the concept.

Part B: connect the sensors, from model to twin

The bridge is now built. Trucks cross it every 10 seconds and a weigh-in-motion sensor reports each truck's weight. The physical bridge below has its own true stiffness, which the model cannot see. Follow the steps and watch the status change from digital model to shadow to twin.

  1. Connect the sensors. Measured deflection streams into the model automatically.
  2. Hidden damage: corrosion takes 20 % of the stiffness. The model is not told.
  3. Recalibrate: estimate the stiffness from measured deflection under known truck weights.

What just happened. With sensors connected, data flowed automatically from object to model: a digital shadow. The residual, the gap between what the physics predicts and what is measured, revealed damage nobody had reported. Recalibration made the model true to this bridge again. Only when the model's conclusion (a load limit) flowed back and changed the real bridge did it become a digital twin in the strict sense.

10 · Check your understanding

Quick quiz

Questions for discussion

  • Bachelor'sPick an everyday object (a lift, a bicycle, a heat pump). What would its physical part, virtual part and connection be? Which decisions could the twin improve?
  • Bachelor'sA company shows a 3D model of its factory that updates once a month from spreadsheets. Is it a model, a shadow or a twin? Why?
  • Master'sAt what point in the lifecycle does a Phase 0 model become a twin? Defend your answer using both Grieves and Vickers (2017) and Kritzinger et al. (2018).
  • Master'sIn the live lab, the twin detected damage from a stiffness change. Which other failure modes would produce no residual in deflection, and what sensors would you add?
  • DoctoralHow should uncertainty in the model (parameters, model form, sensor noise) be quantified and carried into the decisions a twin recommends? Discuss Bayesian calibration and data assimilation.
  • DoctoralPhysics-based models are trustworthy but slow; data-driven models are fast but can fail outside their training data. Compare reduced-order models and physics-informed neural networks (Raissi et al., 2019) as a middle path.
